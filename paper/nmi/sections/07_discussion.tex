\section*{Discussion}
Tool-using agents in our experiments rarely failed to notice a wrong database value. They failed to act on what they noticed.
Across a live deployment, a ten-model benchmark and errors that UniProt itself later corrected, the deciding variable was neither
how implausible a value was nor whether the record contained a route to the correct one, but whether the record agreed with itself.
A value contradicted by another field was often overridden. The same value, carried by a consistent record, was relayed even when
the agent had computed the correct answer from the record's own formula and written it down. Real errors arrive in this second
form: a superseded sequence is consistent with its own length and mass, and 90--100\% of such values passed through every model we
tested, whatever the size of the revision.

Agents increasingly sit between such databases and downstream analyses, and their answers are often read by programs rather than people. In that setting a raised concern is easy to lose: the answer field carried the record's value, while the correct value, when the agent had found it, sat in a separate remark.

Work on knowledge conflicts has shown that language models adopt incorrect context readily, even against their own memory~\cite{wu2024clasheval,xu2024conflicts,xie2024adaptive}, and concurrent work shows that tool-using agents adopt corrupted tool returns and may question a return while passing it on~\cite{yang2026overreliance}. Those studies corrupt single returned values. Our results identify what governs the outcome when the value sits in a structured record: agreement among the record's fields. Agreement can be varied without touching the wrong value, and doing so moved relay by up to 32 percentage points; an error can be made invisible to derivation checks by recomputing the fields around it; and errors that actually occurred in a production database arrive in exactly this form. Values that looked like placeholders were replaced, so implausibility matters at the extreme, but within the range of values that look like data, consistency decides, and the derivation route mattered for detection rather than for the answer. Why agreement dominates, whether it reflects a trained deference to tool outputs or a weighting of evidence by how many sources repeat it, is the mechanistic question this opens; it is testable with prompts that rank the formula above the stated fields or warn the agent that the database contains errors.

Three implications follow for systems that put agents on scientific databases. First, verification has to happen outside the
model and act on the record. A one-sentence instruction to verify reduced relay of impossible values but left plausible ones largely
untouched; derivation checks and an independent second source removed most errors, but only by changing what the agent saw. Second,
how a correction is presented matters as much as whether it is made. Showing the agent both values reopened a decision that silent
correction had closed: one model chose the value labelled as the original in 41\% of runs, and others resolved the conflict from a
memory that, for revised entries, held the superseded value. Reliability labels on retrieved content modestly reduce adoption~\citep{yang2026overreliance};
a label identifying the original database value, by contrast, raised relay in one model. The design principle is to resolve conflicts before the model sees them: correcting silently from a high-agreement source, with the audit trail kept for people outside the agent's context, removed relay entirely in our tests, and transparency toward the people responsible for the analysis is unaffected. Third, a second source must be chosen for agreement and coverage, not convenience. A source that disagrees with the
primary database even 5\% of the time would, applied silently, insert errors into clean records, and entities outside its coverage
remain blind spots: the one real error AlphaFold~DB did not cover passed through every model in every condition.

Our study has limits. Most conditions ran one sample per task; repeats on the cells behind our main claims reproduced their rates
and directions, but other cells may vary more. We studied two domains, two primary databases and question formats with a single
checkable answer; open-ended analyses may behave differently. All errors except those in the UniProt archive were constructed, and the historical records carried current annotations (Methods).
The deployment used one agent framework and two models (Methods).
Model behaviour is tied to the versions we tested, all from 2026. Two experiments were designed after earlier results in the same study and registered before they ran; several registered predictions failed, and we report each. Our cue classifier agrees with human consensus at
$\kappa=0.65$--$0.87$, not perfectly.

The agents we studied behaved less like credulous readers than like careful clerks: they noticed discrepancies, noted them, and
recorded what the database said. For a clerk that is often the right policy. For an agent whose answer feeds an analysis, it means
that database errors become analysis errors unless the record itself is corrected.
