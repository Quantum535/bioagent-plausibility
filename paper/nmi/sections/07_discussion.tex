\section*{Discussion}
Tool-using agents in our experiments rarely failed to notice a wrong database value. They failed to act on what they noticed.
Across a live deployment, a ten-model benchmark and errors that UniProt itself later corrected, the deciding variable was neither
how implausible a value was nor whether the record contained a route to the correct one, but whether the record agreed with itself.
A value contradicted by another field was often overridden. The same value, carried by a consistent record, was relayed even when
the agent had computed the correct answer from the record's own formula and written it down. Real errors arrive in this second
form: a superseded sequence is consistent with its own length and mass, and 90--100\% of such values passed through every model we
tested, whatever the size of the revision.

These errors are not rare. Of our 300 benchmark proteins, chosen without regard to their history, 167 had at least one superseded
sequence in UniProt's archive. Agents increasingly sit between such databases and downstream analyses, and their answers are often
read by programs rather than people. In that setting a raised concern is easy to lose: the answer field carried the record's value,
while the correct value, when the agent had found it, sat in a separate remark.

Work on knowledge conflicts has shown that language models adopt incorrect context readily, less so the further it departs from
the truth~\citep{wu2024clasheval}, and that they accept coherent, convincing counter-evidence even against their own
memory~\citep{xie2024adaptive,xu2024conflicts}. Concurrent work finds that tool-using agents adopt corrupted tool returns and may
question a return, or state the correct value, without warning the user~\citep{yang2026overreliance}. Our results carry these
findings into structured scientific records and identify what decides the outcome there. Values that looked like placeholders were
replaced, consistent with a role for implausibility at the extreme, but within the range of values that look like data the deciding
variable was agreement among the record's fields. Agreement can be varied without changing the wrong value at all, and doing so moved
relay by up to 32 percentage points. The derivation route, which we expected to protect values, mattered mainly for detection: it let
agents see the error and say so, but not change their answer.

Three implications follow for systems that put agents on scientific databases. First, verification has to happen outside the
model and act on the record. A one-sentence instruction to verify reduced relay of impossible values but left plausible ones largely
untouched; derivation checks and an independent second source removed most errors, but only by changing what the agent saw. Second,
how a correction is presented matters as much as whether it is made. Showing the agent both values reopened a decision that silent
correction had closed: one model chose the value labelled as the original in 41\% of runs, and others resolved the conflict from a
memory that, for revised entries, held the superseded value. Reliability labels on retrieved content modestly reduce adoption~\citep{yang2026overreliance};
a label identifying the original database value, by contrast, raised relay in one model. Correcting silently from a high-agreement source, and keeping the
audit trail for people outside the agent's context, removed relay entirely in our tests. This cuts against an instinct for
transparency toward the model, and the trade-off deserves study; transparency toward the people responsible for the analysis is
unaffected. Third, a second source must be chosen for agreement and coverage, not convenience. A source that disagrees with the
primary database even 5\% of the time would, applied silently, insert errors into clean records, and entities outside its coverage
remain blind spots: the one real error AlphaFold~DB did not cover passed through every model in every condition.

Our study has limits. Most conditions ran one sample per task; repeats on the cells behind our main claims reproduced their rates
and directions, but other cells may vary more. We studied two domains, two primary databases and question formats with a single
checkable answer; open-ended analyses may behave differently. All errors except those in the UniProt archive were constructed, and
the historical records combined superseded sequences with current annotations, because historical entries are not archived in full.
The deployment used one agent framework and two models, because the Claude models could not run in that framework's agent loop.
Model behaviour is tied to the versions we tested, all from 2026. Two experiments, the agreement follow-up and the comparison of
presentations, were designed after earlier results in the same study and registered before they ran; several registered
predictions failed or could not be tested, and we report each. Our cue classifier agrees with human consensus at
$\kappa=0.65$--$0.87$, not perfectly. During the study we found and corrected three collisions in our run cache; all affected runs
were repeated, and a run-level check confirmed that every corrupted run received the record it was meant to (Methods).

The agents we studied behaved less like credulous readers than like careful clerks: they noticed discrepancies, noted them, and
recorded what the database said. For a clerk that is often the right policy. For an agent whose answer feeds an analysis, it means
that database errors become analysis errors unless the record itself is corrected.
